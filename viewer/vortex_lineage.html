Vortex Lineage

Each horn torus carries one vortex. Its spike curves out and ends in the horn point of the next torus, its child. After four generations the last spike lands in the parent's own horn point, so the parent is its own great-great-grandchild. The −1 children do the same in mirror image: four blue tori at the opposite corners, each antivortex feeding the next, closing after four. Together the two lineages fill the eight corners of a cube. The bow slider takes the spikes from straight edges to arcs bowed out onto a sphere.

What this shows

  • The mirror lineage behaves the same way, reflected. Reflecting the whole +1 lineage through the center gives the −1 lineage: every corner goes to the opposite corner, every vortex becomes an antivortex, and its loop closes after four in the same way. The total charge of the whole structure is 0: four +1 and four −1.
  • The lineage closes after four. Parent, child, grandchild, great-grandchild, and then the fourth spike lands in the parent's horn point. Following one spike at a time, you are home after four.
  • Each torus stays balanced. Every torus sends one unit of curl out through its vortex and receives one through its horn point, so its charges add to zero, as a torus requires. The curl is never created or lost; it travels around the loop.
  • The angles. From the center, any two corners are 109.47° apart (the tetrahedral angle). Where a spike arrives and the next one leaves, the corner angle is 60° when the spikes are straight edges. When they bow out onto the sphere, it opens to 120°. That extra opening is what the sphere's positive curvature does.
  • The loop uses four of the tetrahedron's six edges. It is a skew square, and the two edges it skips are dashed. Stepping once around it is a quarter turn combined with a mirror. That's the same four-step cycle found on the Four-Flag Tetrahedron and Curved Successions pages.
