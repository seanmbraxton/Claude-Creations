Surface Vortices

The parent torus is painted by phase: each color is one direction on the color wheel. Where every color meets at a point, the surface has a local vortex, a curl around that point rather than around the torus. Each vortex rises as one spike into a child horn torus, which is a close-up of that vortex.

What this shows

  • A vortex is local curl. Around it, the phase turns once through every color, but a small loop around a point can shrink to nothing on the surface. So the windings (t, p), which count trips around the torus, never register it.
  • Each child horn torus is a close-up of one vortex. Near a horn point, the angle around the torus becomes the angle around the point. The spike is the vortex core, where the phase is undetermined.
  • On a plain torus, vortices must cancel. With no twist, the charges always add to zero (the Poincaré–Hopf theorem; a torus has Euler characteristic 0). That's why you can only add or remove them in +1/−1 pairs.
  • A twist forces vortices. Turn the twist up to n and the field needs n unpaired vortices. The colors along the top and bottom edges of the flat map then cycle n times. That's the twist itself, and no smoothing can remove it. Physically, it's like n units of magnetic flux through the surface.
  • Two different counts. The windings (t, p) say how a path goes around the torus. The phase turns along the path change by one each time a vortex crosses it. Moving a vortex changes the second count and never the first.
  • Where the number theory is. The coloring is built from theta functions, and their zeros are the vortices. The simplest theta function is 1 + 2q + 2q⁴ + 2q⁹ + …, one term per square number. Its powers count the ways to write a number as a sum of squares.
